# Floor slab

Specified 2026-08-16. Concrete ground-bearing slab inside the timber frame,
structurally separate from it.

## The levels govern, not the loads

| | |
|---|---|
| Outside grade | 0 mm |
| **Sill underside** (pile top) | **200 mm** — the hard ceiling |
| Sill top / post foot | 570 mm |

Anything built up inside has to finish **at or below 200 mm at the wall line**,
or the sill gets buried and the ventilated underside is lost.

## The "5 cm layer between floor and wall" is an air gap

You want the underside of the wall open so it can dry in summer. Fill that gap
with 50 mm of foam and you have closed the very thing you asked for. **The two
requirements are the same requirement.**

**Finish the slab top at 150 mm.** That leaves a continuous **50 mm slot under the
whole sill**, 150 mm deep (the wall thickness), open to outside between the piles.

One move, four jobs:

1. The slab never touches timber anywhere
2. The void vents inside-to-outside all round
3. No capillary path, because there is no contact
4. The slab can shrink and heave without levering the frame (F23)

Still leave **20 mm horizontally** at the slab edge with a compressible closed-cell
strip. That is a **movement joint, not a moisture one** — concrete shrinks about
0.5 mm/m, so 9.31 m of slab moves 4.7 mm before thermal. The moisture job is done
by the air gap above it.

## Build-up, top down

| Layer | Thickness | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Slab, C25/30, A252 mesh at mid-depth | **150 mm** | top at +150 |
| DPM, 0.3 mm (1200 gauge) polythene | — | lap 300, tape, turn **up** at edges |
| Blinding, sand or crushed fines | 50 mm | compacted |
| Hardcore — your rock and brick | 150 mm | 150 mm lifts, each compacted |
| Formation level | | −200 mm |

**Dig 200 mm below grade** inside the footprint. That is the real constraint — the
10 cm of rock is the easy part.

## Thickness — judged on the corner, not the middle

A slab is weakest at a free edge and weakest of all at a **corner**, and the
entrance is exactly that. Under a 10 kN machine foot, C25/30 allowable 1.73 MPa:

| Slab | Interior | Edge | **Corner** | |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 70 mm | 1.05 | 1.07 | **2.63** | fails |
| 100 mm | 0.55 | 0.59 | **1.54** | passes, nothing spare |
| **125 mm** | 0.37 | 0.40 | **1.08** | minimum |
| **150 mm** | 0.26 | 0.30 | **0.79** | recommended |

Punching is never critical — 57 to 124 kN against a 10 kN foot.

**Thicken to 200 mm for 1.5 m in from the entrance.** Free corner, and every wheel
load in the building lands there. The doors are automatic and hang off the wall and
ties, so they put almost nothing on the floor themselves.

## The DPM also saves you from the brick

Old brick and mortar carry **sulfates**. In wet ground they attack ordinary
Portland cement from below: the slab expands, cracks and delaminates, and it takes
years to show. A membrane directly under the slab breaks that path as well as the
moisture one. **Do not skip it to save €60.**

Rules for the fill:

- **Clean brick and stone only.** No plaster, gypsum, plasterboard, wood or soil.
  Gypsum is the worst sulfate source there is.
- **150 mm lifts, compacted each time.** One dumped 300 mm layer settles later and
  cracks the slab.
- **Blind it.** 50–200 mm rock has huge voids; concrete poured straight on loses
  depth into the gaps and the mesh ends up sitting on the stone.

## Reinforcement — mesh, not loose rods

**A252**: 8 mm bars at 200 mm both ways, 252 mm²/m.

If you are using loose rod, tie it to the same grid and get it at **mid-depth on
chairs**. Mesh lying on the blinding does nothing at all — it has to be in the
tension zone.

## Driving a car on it

**Yes — the slab is not the constraint.** Westergaard on 150 mm C25/30, allowable
1.73 MPa. The worst case is always the free **corner**, which is exactly where you
drive in:

| Vehicle | Wheel | Interior | Edge | **Corner** | |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Car, 1600 kg | 4 kN | 0.10 | 0.12 | **0.30** | OK |
| Car, 2000 kg | 5 kN | 0.13 | 0.14 | **0.38** | 4.6× |
| Loaded van, 3500 kg | 10 kN | 0.26 | 0.29 | **0.75** | 2.3× |
| Truck axle | 20 kN | 0.52 | 0.58 | **1.50** | inside |
| Mixer wheel | 40 kN | 1.04 | 1.16 | **3.01** | over, at a corner |

In the 200 mm entrance zone a van corner is 0.46 MPa — factor 3.7. Punching never
governs (151 kN against a 10 kN wheel) and the load spreads to 49 kPa on the
subgrade.

### But you cannot get a car in without a ramp

The floor is **250 mm above outside grade** — the whole point of raising it 50 above
the sill. A car cannot climb a 250 mm step, and the 0.50 m apron is a **1:2
gradient: a kerb, not a ramp.**

| Gradient | Ramp length | |
|---|---|---|
| 1:6 | 1.50 m | too steep, most cars ground out |
| **1:8** | **2.00 m** | steep but usable |
| **1:10** | **2.50 m** | comfortable |
| 1:12 | 3.00 m | comfortable loaded or with a trailer |

Two details the ramp has to get right:

**Breakover at the top.** Ease the transition over the last 300–500 mm — a ramp
meeting the level floor at a sharp angle catches the belly of anything long and low.

**Drainage.** A ramp falling toward the building funnels rain straight at the
opening. Fall it **away** at the bottom, or put a channel drain across the
threshold. This is the one that does damage, and the entrance is the wall with no
sill and no piers under it (F8).

### And check it fits

Internal floor **9.01 × 5.70 m**, entrance 3.0 m wide. An estate parked centrally
leaves 1.90 m each side — a walkway, not a bench. A car fits; a car *plus* a
workshop is the real question.

## Still open

**The entrance jambs.** F23: the frame is bolted to 1.2 m piles, the slab bears at
grade. The slab detail above stops the slab levering the frame, but if the two jamb
posts stand on the slab they still move seasonally while everything else does not —
at the most heavily loaded, least supported point in the building. **Put 2 more
piles under them.**
