# Architect's review

Dārza iela 10, Sigulda · reviewed 2026-08-14 · **intended use: workshop**

Everything so far has asked "will it stand up". This asks "will it be a good
building to work in", which is a different question and turns up different
answers. The structure is in decent shape. The *building* has four gaps, and
none of them is structural.

---

## 1. There is no floor, and that is now the biggest gap

A store can live on beaten earth. A workshop cannot — you need a flat, dry,
level surface that takes a machine on castors and does not wick damp into
everything standing on it.

The approved design specifies **betona grīda**. Nothing is built.

**A timber floor cannot span the 6 m between sills:**

| Joist | 6.0 m span | 3.0 m span |
|---|---|---|
| 200 × 50 @ 400 | 55 mm | 3.5 mm |
| 250 × 50 @ 400 | 28 mm | 1.8 mm |

Limit is 10 mm. So a suspended floor needs a **sleeper line down the middle** —
which is easy, because sleeper piers carry only the floor and are independent
of the frame.

**But for a workshop I would pour the slab**, for three reasons a timber floor
cannot match: point loads from machinery, no bounce when you are working, and
a mass that damps vibration.

**Critical**: keep the slab **structurally separate from the frame**. The frame
sits on piers that will settle; a slab on hardcore will not settle the same way.
Pour it inside the sills with a compressible perimeter joint, so the two move
independently. Never tie the slab to the sill.

---

## 2. Condensation is the detail that ruins metal-roofed workshops

Warm moist air from a workshop rises, meets cold steel, and condenses on the
underside. It then drips onto whatever is below. This is the single most-missed
detail on buildings like this one, and it is much cheaper to design out than to
retrofit.

**Fix, in order of importance:**

- **Anti-condensation membrane** (fleece-backed sheet) or a breather membrane
  directly under the sheeting
- **Ventilated cavity** — counter-battens over the membrane, so air moves from
  eaves to ridge
- **Vented ridge and eaves.** The 0.53 m eaves overhang is ideal for an
  intake vent; the ridge needs an outlet
- If you board the loft ceiling, **ventilate the roof void above it** and the
  problem largely goes away, because the cold void never sees workshop air

Do not fix sheeting straight onto rafters with nothing between.

---

## 3. No daylight

Zero windows at present. A workshop wants **10–20% of floor area** as glazing;
on 55.9 m² that is 5.6–11.2 m².

**Six bays at 1.0 × 1.2 m gives 7.2 m², or 13%.** That works well here because
the wall braces sit in the **end bays** — mid-bay windows cost no racking
capacity at all. See F10 before cutting anything: the braces terminate at the
girt, so keep openings clear of those bays.

Orientation matters more than area. North light is even and shadow-free, which
is what you want over a bench. South light is bright and will bake the space
under a metal roof in summer. If the bench goes on one wall, glaze the opposite
one so you are not working in your own shadow.

The 3 m entrance is a good workshop feature — machinery goes in, long stock
comes in — but it faces one way only and is the structural weak point (F8).

---

## 4. Loft: decide now, because it changes the ties

**Ceiling only:** 22.6 mm final deflection against a 24 mm limit. Fine.

**Workshop storage:** 45.9 mm. Nearly double the limit.

A workshop will accumulate storage — that is what workshops do. If you want to
use the loft properly, the spine beam has to go in **before the deck**, and it
needs posts to the ground, which costs floor space. Decide before the deck is
laid, because retrofitting under a boarded floor is miserable work.

---

## Smaller things, in rough order

- **Loft access.** No stair or ladder is designed. A fixed ladder needs about
  1.2 × 0.6 m of floor; a proper stair needs 3–4 m². Nothing is allowed for.
- **Heating.** If you heat it, service class moves from 2 to 1 and creep drops
  (kdef 0.80 → 0.60). But heating an uninsulated frame on open piers with no
  floor is pointless — floor and insulation come first, in that order.
- **Dust and fumes.** Openable windows plus a high-level vent. A metal roof
  workshop with no ventilation is unbearable in July.
- **Timber treatment.** The permit requires insecticide **and fire retardant**.
  A workshop raises the fire duty — solvents, sawdust, electrical load.
- **Power.** Permit allows an electricity connection. Plan circuits before
  cladding goes on; surface conduit later is ugly and vulnerable.

---

## Use versus permit

The permit describes **noliktava dārza instrumentiem** — a garden tool store.
A workshop is a different use. For a private, non-commercial workshop in a
Group I auxiliary building this is usually unremarkable, but it is a change
from what was described, and it sits alongside two other deviations already on
record: the foundation and the roof height.

Worth a conversation with Sigulda būvvalde rather than a surprise at handover.
